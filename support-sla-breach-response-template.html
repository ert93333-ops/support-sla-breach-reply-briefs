What to confirm before a missed-SLA update goes out

  1. Summarize customer impact. Say who was affected, what workflow was blocked, and what customer-visible delay happened.
  2. Name the SLA or promise. Include plan, priority, response-window, or customer expectation context without pasting contract text.
  3. Add the timeline. Record request time, promised response time, missed update time, breach duration, and current status.
  4. Translate the cause. Replace internal jargon with a customer-readable cause.
  5. Own the miss. Include apology and ownership tone instead of vague inconvenience language.
  6. Give the next update time. Do not send without a specific update time, cadence, or deadline.
  7. Name the remediation owner. Assign support, CS, engineering, TAM, incident, or account ownership before sending.
  8. Resolve escalation path. Define who handles escalation and what trigger moves the case.
  9. Separate credit or refund review. Do not promise compensation without the right internal approval.
  10. Remove risky language. Avoid blame, minimizing words, unnecessary jargon, and promises that it will never happen again.
  11. Record internal follow-up. Keep a send/hold decision, owner, review date, postmortem step, or policy follow-up.

Support SLA Breach Reply Briefs vs Zendesk macros

Zendesk macros and help-desk snippets can speed replies, but they often miss incident-specific customer impact, timeline, plain-language cause, next update time, remediation owner, escalation path, internal review ambiguity, and follow-up decisions.

This MVP stays browser-local and works from pasted public-safe notes. It does not connect to help desks, send customer messages, interpret contracts, advise on legal liability, promise refunds or credits, validate deliverability, replace company policy, or store ticket data remotely.
